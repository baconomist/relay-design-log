The need

Too many patients, too few monitors, all on paper

Shambhala's medical tent is run by about 250 volunteers and sees about 2,500 visits a week. About 1,800 of those are seen by a provider and about 700 end up in beds, up to 200 a day at peak. Monitors are limited and shared between patients, so vitals get missed, and everything between the RFID wristband scans at arrival and discharge is charted on paper and typed up by hand after the festival.

Case in point: the tent has about 10 beds but only 6 monitors, and some charts show just 2 sets of vitals over 6 hours. Typing up the paper charts takes 20 to 25 med students plus about 30 hours of the medical director's own time every season.

Objective

Get more patients monitored with the gear and people they have

Build a vitals system around off-the-shelf, already-licensed sensors that can spot-check a patient at triage or stay on them in bed. Readings go through a local gateway that works without internet, attach to the patient's existing RFID encounter ID, land straight in the chart, and feed a live bed board that flags overdue vitals for the twice-daily safety briefings.

Constraints

What the solution has to live with

  • It must work without internet, since connectivity on site is patchy.
  • Every reading must be tied to a patient or bed ID, and stay correct when patients switch beds.
  • It must be rugged. The tent's $50k defibrillators and monitors still break on site.
  • It must be simpler than paper for volunteer staff. Charting compliance dropped the last time the tent tried electronic records.
Required vitals

What it has to measure, in the paramedics' priority order

Must have

SpO2 with the pulse waveform, and heart rate. ECG is useful in some cases but not required.

Next

Blood pressure, which needs specialized equipment, and respiratory rate, which can come from impedance.

Later

Glucose, and maybe temperature: it often doesn't get taken, but only about 5 patients a year are out of range.

Also asked for

Movement alerts when a patient wakes up and starts pulling out their IV, and a geofence for patients trying to leave.

Prior art

Alternatives considered

Why not build our own sensor, or use smartwatches?

  • Licensing our own sensor as a Class II device costs little in Health Canada fees (C$643 to apply) but roughly $300k to $1M+ and 1 to 2 years for the quality system, safety testing and accuracy studies.
  • The "six Apple Watches" idea doesn't hold up: no blood pressure, no continuous SpO2, one iPhone per watch, and a post-surgery trial found its SpO2 much worse than clinical monitors.
  • No wearable reliably detects dehydration yet, and blood sodium still needs a fingerprick test like Abbott's i-STAT, which could feed into the same record later.
Open risk

Risks we're designing around

Alerts could make it a medical device

We framed it as a documentation aid to stay outside medical device rules, but staff want alerts for IV pulling and patients leaving. How far alerts can go before the software needs licensing is still open.

Volunteers may not use it

Charting compliance dropped the last time the tent tried electronic records.

Mitigation: vitals log automatically from the sensors, so nobody has to type into a phone.

Wearables can walk off

A patient can leave with a sensor still on. Staff already want a geofence alert for patients trying to leave; how that would work is still open.

Alerts have to reach someone

Staff mostly coordinate over radio today, so a flag on a screen can go unseen. Who gets notified, and how, is still open.
